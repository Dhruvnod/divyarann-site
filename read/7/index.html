Chapter Seven

Drishti

दृष्टि

At six minutes past eleven, the glass stopped humming.

Kairavi noticed the silence before she noticed anything else, because the hum had been there for nine days and her body had built itself around it. She looked up from the laptop. Aarohi, across the table, looked up at the same moment, and for a second the two of them simply looked at each other, the way people do when a sound they have stopped hearing stops.

Then the window bowed.

It did not shatter. It curved inward, the whole pane, slow and smooth, like a soap film pressed by a finger, and held there. Kairavi had time to think that is not how glass behaves before the pressure arrived in her ears and her thought became a single high whine.

Her phone lit up. Aarohi’s lit up. From the kitchen, Meera’s lit up, and Meera said, “Kairavi?” in a voice that had no question in it.

She stood. She did not remember deciding to stand. She crossed to the window, and the pressure in her ears climbed with every step, and she put her hand flat against the glass and felt it trembling under her palm like something alive and afraid.

The sky over Dwarka was folding.

She had spent nine days building models of this moment, and not one of them had contained the word folding. The blue above the rooftops had creased along a line that ran from the metro corridor in the east to somewhere beyond the sector boundary in the west, and the two halves were drawing together along it, and the light along the crease was wrong, bent, as though it were passing through water.

“Didi.”

“I see it.”

“Didi, what is it?”

“I don’t know.”

The crease split.

Afterwards, she would try to describe the sound to people who had been indoors, and she would fail every time. It was not loud. It was low, lower than hearing, a single beat that came up through the floor and the soles of her feet and the chair legs and the table, and set every loose object in the flat trembling in sympathy. The biscuit tin on the shoe rack chattered against the torch on top of it.

Where the crease had been, there was a tear, and behind the tear there was dark.

The dark moved. It was not far away. That was the thing she could not fit into any model: the sky should have been far away, and this was close, close enough that she could see texture in it, close enough that when the first shape detached from the dark and fell, she could follow it with her eyes all the way down.

It fell towards the market. Towards Sector 12. A column of dust went up where it landed.

A second shape fell further east. A third, further still. The sky was letting things go the way a tree lets go of fruit, a few at a time, without hurry.

Her phone buzzed. Mitali: Are you seeing this. Where are you. Stay inside.

Then the signal bars went from four to none.

Kairavi turned from the window.

Meera stood in the kitchen doorway with a ladle in her hand. Aarohi had both hands flat on the table as if to hold it down. Neither of them was looking at the sky any more. They were looking at her.

She understood, in that moment, several things at once, and later she would recognise it as the first time the understanding had come to her in that form: not in sequence, but all together, as a shape.

The plan said the metro station. Rajeev had said before the crowds. The crowds would begin the moment the first shock passed and people realised their phones were dead. The lift could not be trusted. The stairs could. The flat was on the fourth floor of a building whose window had just bent without breaking, and she did not know what that meant for the walls.

“We go now,” she said. “Shoes. Maa, the tin. Aarohi, both torches and the water. Nothing else.”

“Your father—”

“Papa said he would reach the station before anyone. He meant it. Shoes.”

Meera put down the ladle.

Kairavi’s phone buzzed once more, a single message that had fought its way through whatever was left of the network. It was from her father, and it was three words long.

Going to station. Come.

She showed it to her mother without a word, and Meera made a sound that was half laugh and half something else, and went to get her shoes.

The lift had finally stopped lying about where it was. It stood between the second and first floors with its doors half open on a wall of concrete and nobody inside, and they went past it down the stairs.

The stairwell was full of people and the people were full of questions. Nobody had answers. A man in a vest held his phone above his head as if the signal might be hiding near the ceiling. Two children were crying in the particular rhythm of children who have caught fear from their parents and do not know what it is for. Mrs Bhalla from the second floor was standing on her landing in a nightgown with her grandson’s hand in hers, and she said, “Kairavi, beta, what is it?” in the same tone Aarohi had used, as if Kairavi were the one person in the building who would know.

“We’re going to the metro station,” Kairavi said. “The pillars are strong. Come with us.”

“My son is at work—”

“Then he will look for you at the station. Everyone will. Come.”

Mrs Bhalla came. So did the grandson, who was eight and whose name was Veer, and a delivery rider in a red jacket who had been trapped in the building when the lift stopped and who fell in beside them at the ground floor without asking, as if a group with a direction were the only thing in the world worth joining.

Outside, the street had changed.

Not broken. Changed. The traffic along the sector road had locked solid in both directions, and the drivers were abandoning their cars where they stood, so that the road had become a field of open doors. A scooter lay on its side with its wheel still turning. People were running, and Kairavi watched them run for the length of one breath and saw that they were running in both directions, which meant nothing. It meant nobody knew where away was.

The air pressed on her. Not the way it had at the window. Steadily, from above, as if the sky had gained weight.

“Which way?” Aarohi said.

The station was east. Fifteen minutes on an ordinary day, down the sector road to the junction, across, and along the service lane under the metro corridor. Kairavi looked east along the sector road, and that was when she saw the first one.

It stood at the junction.

She would never be able to say how tall it was. Taller than the auto-rickshaws it stood among, which it had not pushed aside so much as walked through. Its proportions were wrong in a way her eye kept trying to correct: the shoulders too massive for the body, the head sunk too low between them, every joint bent a few degrees past where a joint should bend. Its surface had the black and the dull sheen of cooled slag, and deep in its cracks something glowed like a fire not quite out. It had a face. That was the worst of it. It was a face off a temple wall, the eyes round and burning, a collar of bronze sunk into the flesh of its neck, as though whatever had made it had been working from a description of a carving given by someone who had only heard about one.

It was not doing anything. It stood in the junction with its head low between its shoulders and the abandoned cars around it, and the people who had been running towards the junction were now running away from it, and the people running from the other direction had not seen it yet.

“Back,” Kairavi said. “Behind the bus.”

A DTC bus stood dead at the kerb with its doors open. They went behind it, all six of them, and Kairavi put her back against its warm, dusty flank and made herself breathe.

Think. It was the only thing she had ever been good at that was worth anything now.

The station was on the other side of the junction. There was no other way to it that did not go through open ground, and open ground meant being seen by the thing in the junction, and she did not know what being seen by it meant.

She looked.

Carefully, around the back of the bus, with one eye. She looked at the thing the way she would have looked at a failing model: not at what it was, but at what it did.

It moved.

Not towards anything. It took three steps, fast, faster than its proportions should have allowed, and the steps carried it halfway across the junction to a car whose alarm had begun to sound. It struck the car once. The alarm stopped. The car was no longer a car.

Then it stopped. It stood still, with its head turned slightly, and Kairavi counted, because counting was what she had, and she reached four before it moved again.

Another three steps. Another stop. The head turned again.

“It’s listening,” she said.

“What?” Mrs Bhalla’s voice was very small.

“It moves and then it stops and listens. It goes to sound.” She watched it do it a third time. The glass in the dead cars around the junction was trembling, and it trembled more when the thing was still than when it moved, and she filed that away without knowing what to do with it. “The alarm. It went to the alarm.”

“Then we stay quiet and we stay here,” the delivery rider said.

“No.” The word came out before the reasoning did, and she made herself say the reasoning anyway, for Aarohi, for Meera. “More people are coming from the west. They will reach this junction in a few minutes. They haven’t seen it. They will be loud. And when they come, it will be between us and the station with a crowd around it, and we will not get through.”

“Then what?”

She did not know. She knew she had to know. The thing in the junction did not care that she was an AI engineer who had once wanted to study the stars, and did not care that her mother was behind her with a biscuit tin full of documents, and did not care that she had been right about the acceleration. It would do what it did. The only question was whether she could understand what it did well enough to get six people past it.

Understand one thing. One thing, and act on it.

She stepped out from behind the bus.

“Kairavi!” Meera’s hand caught at her sleeve and missed.

She needed to see the whole junction, not a slice of it. She needed to see where the thing stood in relation to the cars, the lane, the pillars of the metro corridor beyond, the people coming from the west. She stood in the open with her heart going like a trapped bird and looked, and the thing in the junction turned its burning eyes towards the sound of her mother’s voice, and the pressure above her came down like a hand.

And the street lit.

Not with light. With lines.

They were everywhere, all at once, thin and bright and absolutely silent, and they were the same lines she had seen on the office glass and the window frame, except that now they did not fade. They ran along the sector road in long taut cables of tension. They clustered at the base of a balcony on the building beside the junction, a dense knot of them, pulling downward. They threaded through the crowd approaching from the west, bright where one body pulled against another and dark where gaps opened between them. They spilled from the dead cars in loose tangles.

And around the thing in the junction, they did something she had no word for.

They bent towards it. All of them, from every direction, the way iron filings bend towards a magnet, and where they reached it they did not touch it but stopped short, as if it stood inside a space the lines could not enter. When it moved—three fast steps—the bent lines snapped straight behind it like released elastic and shivered. When it stopped, they bent again, slowly, and in the bending she saw it: the thing was not listening. It was pulling. Everything around it was being drawn towards it, sound and glass and air, and it moved to wherever the pull was strongest, and the pull was strongest wherever something was shaking.

The pain arrived a second after the sight, as though it had been following at a distance.

It went in behind her eyes and did not come out. The lines doubled. The street doubled. She saw the junction and its skeleton superimposed and could not make them agree, and she put a hand against the bus to keep the ground where it was.

“Didi.” Aarohi was beside her. “Didi, your nose.”

She wiped it with the back of her hand and looked at the blood and put the hand down and looked at the junction again, because she had not finished.

The thing moved. Three steps. Stop. And in the stop, the lines bent, and the bending took time: a slow inhale, four counts, before it went again. In those four counts everything near it shivered towards it. A man who stepped on a broken bottle in those four counts would be heard. A child who cried would be heard.

But in the three steps, with the lines snapping straight behind it, the thing was committed. It was going where it was going. It could not hear anything but its own going.

“We move when it moves,” she said. “Not when it stops. Only when it moves.”

“That’s backwards,” the delivery rider said.

“Yes.”

He looked at her face, and at the blood, and whatever he saw there made him close his mouth.

She watched the thing finish its stop. It went. Three steps, west, towards the sound of the approaching crowd, and she said, “Now,” and they ran.

Not far. Thirty metres, from the bus to the shell of a van that stood nearer the junction, and they reached it as the thing stopped, and she pressed Veer’s face against her side and held her own breath and counted four in her head with the lines bending all around her like grass in a wind she could not feel.

It went again. “Now.”

Twenty metres. Behind the van, a line of parked autos. They reached them. Stop. Four. The lines bent. Mrs Bhalla was making a sound in her throat on every exhale and Kairavi put a hand over the old woman’s mouth without apologising, and Mrs Bhalla’s eyes above the hand understood, and the sound stopped.

The thing went again, and this time it went east, towards them, and Kairavi felt the whole group tense to run, and said, “No. Hold.”

It passed within ten metres of the autos. The pressure of its passing pulled at her clothes. The lines behind it snapped straight with a shiver she felt in her teeth. It stopped on the far side of them, between them and the bus they had left, and in the junction ahead, for the length of one stop, there was nothing between them and the service lane but open road and the balcony she had seen with the knot of lines beneath it.

“When it goes,” she said, “we cross. Stay off the pavement on the right. Stay in the road.”

“The pavement is—”

“Stay in the road.”

The thing went. “Now.”

They crossed the junction in the open, six people in a ragged line, and from the west a man broke from the approaching crowd and sprinted for the same service lane, and he was on the pavement, the right-hand pavement, and he was fast, and the thing behind them stopped.

Kairavi did not see what happened to him. She heard the stop. She heard the lines bend, a sound that was not a sound. She heard the man’s feet on the pavement, loud, louder than anything, and then she heard three steps that were not his, and then she heard nothing from him at all.

Aarohi had turned to look. Kairavi took her sister’s face in her hands and turned it forward and said, “Lane. Go,” and Aarohi went.

They were in the mouth of the service lane when the balcony came down. It came down exactly where the knot had been, onto the right-hand pavement where a man would have been standing if he had listened, in a slow sliding roar of concrete and dust, and the sound of it was enormous, and behind them in the junction the thing turned its whole wrong body towards the sound and went to it, three steps, three steps, three steps, away from them.

She had known the balcony would fall. She had known it the way she knew a model would fail: not when, but that.

She had not known about the man.

The service lane ran under the metro corridor, between great concrete pillars stained by years of rain and traffic. She knew only that the pillars were strong, because the lines along them were long and even and did not bend, and that she could no longer see properly.

It was not that the lines had stopped. It was that they had not. They were in everything now. Every pillar, every parked scooter, every person running past, every crack in the lane’s surface was drawn over with its own bright skeleton, and the skeletons did not stay still, and she could not shut them off, and behind her eyes the pain had become a white sheet with no edges.

She walked into a pillar.

Not hard. She simply did not see it, because she saw it too well, and her shoulder struck concrete and she stopped.

“Didi.”

“I can’t—” She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. The lines were there too, on the inside of her lids. “I can’t see the lane. There’s too much.”

Aarohi took her arm.

Nineteen years old, in a T-shirt with a cartoon cat on it, with one torch in her other hand that she had not once turned on. She put Kairavi’s hand on her shoulder the way one guides someone blind, and said, “Then don’t look. I’ll look. Walk,” and Kairavi walked.

It was two hundred metres. It took a long time. She heard Meera behind her, breathing hard. She heard Mrs Bhalla telling Veer that they were nearly there in a voice that did not believe it. She heard the delivery rider say “left, left, there’s a bike” and felt Aarohi steer her. She heard the crowd ahead before she could have seen it, a sound like surf, and under the sound the station’s pillars stood in her mind as the only straight lines in the whole city, and she walked towards them with her eyes closed and her hand on her little sister’s shoulder and did not think about the pavement.

“Steps,” Aarohi said. “Up. Three.”

She went up three.

The sound of the crowd closed over her. It was a hundred voices, more, under a concrete roof, and it had a shape she could hear without looking: people pressed towards the pillars, people shouting names, somewhere a baby. And through it, closer than the rest, one voice she had not heard raised in years.

“Kairavi! Meera! Here! Here!”

She opened her eyes.

The lines were still there. But her father was in front of them, and the lines did not matter.

Rajeev stood between two pillars with a supermarket trolley full of water bottles and the shutter key of his shop still in his fist, as though he had locked up and run and not stopped running. His face was the colour of paper. He looked at Kairavi’s face and at the blood on it and at Aarohi’s hand on her arm, and something went out of him and something else came in, and he did not ask what had happened.

He took hold of Meera first. Then Aarohi. Then, with the two of them still against him, he put out his free hand and closed it around the back of Kairavi’s neck, the way he had done when she was six and had fallen off a cycle, and held her there.

“I said before the crowds,” he said, into her hair. “I said it.”

“You did.”

“I have been here since half past eleven. I have been here twenty minutes.”

“I know, Papa.”

He let go. He looked past her, at Mrs Bhalla and the boy and the delivery rider in his red jacket, and he did not ask who they were. He pulled three bottles out of the trolley and put them into their hands.

Meera set the biscuit tin down on the concrete between two pillars and sat on it, and laughed until she cried.

Kairavi lowered herself to the ground beside her mother and shut her eyes again, because it was still too much, and for a while she simply breathed and listened to the crowd and let Aarohi hold a wet cloth against her face.

That was how she was sitting when she heard the scream.

It was not a human scream. It was not an animal one. It came from the east, from the direction of the market, and it came through the concrete roof and the hundred voices and the white sheet behind her eyes as if none of them were there: a long rising shriek like a wire drawn tight across the whole sky and then struck.

The crowd went silent.

She opened her eyes. Through the gap between the pillars, over the rooftops to the east, through the smoke that was rising from a dozen places now, a ring of light was spreading. Violet at its heart, so deep it was almost black. Gold at its rim. It rolled outward through the smoke in a single clean wave, and where it passed the smoke turned over, and the rooftops lit, and the sound of it arrived a second later as a pressure that pushed every person on the concourse one step back.

Then it was gone, and the smoke closed, and the crowd began to make noise again.

Kairavi did not. She sat with her back against a pillar and her sister’s cloth against her face and looked east, where the ring had been, and saw what nobody else on the concourse saw.

A line. One line, drawn from somewhere along the sector road straight up into the torn sky, taut as a bowstring, and in the whole bent and shivering city, the only line that did not bend at all.

She did not know what it was.

She knew that it had been made.
