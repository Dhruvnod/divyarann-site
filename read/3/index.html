Chapter Three

स्पंदनthe pulse

Raghav, then Kabir · about 15 minutes

You stopped about of the way through.

Eight days had passed since the first unexplained pulse, and Delhi had stopped expecting an immediate explanation.

The apartment had excellent sunlight, terrible bathroom tiles and a balcony just wide enough for Raghav to pretend he would grow plants.

“Herbs,” he said, standing in the doorway. “Maybe tomatoes.”

The broker looked at the empty clay pots on the neighbouring balconies. “Very good sunlight, sir.”

“That is what worries me. Plants become emotionally dependent.”

The broker gave a professional smile. It was the kind reserved for customers who had not yet produced a cheque.

Raghav stepped onto the balcony. Nine floors below, cars moved between the apartment blocks in patient rows. The metro crossed the distance on concrete pillars. Beyond it, Dwarka spread through pale towers, rooftop water tanks and small rectangles of green that builders described as forest-facing.

He had visited the flat three months earlier, when the walls still smelled of fresh paint and the asking price had seemed negotiable. The second bedroom could become an office. The living room had enough space for his father’s old chess table. The parking space was larger than his current hatchback required and exactly right for the Porsche 911 Carrera he planned to own before his knees became too old to enter it with dignity.

He had gone home that day and built a spreadsheet.

Down payment. Registration. Furnishing. Emergency reserve. Expected salary increments. The amount he could save each month without becoming the sort of person who examined restaurant menus from right to left.

The numbers had worked.

Then Samyukt had called.

Raghav had transferred four lakh rupees two days later.

He did not regret it. That was the inconvenient part. Regret would have been simpler than standing inside the life he had postponed and still knowing he would make the same choice again.

“I can arrange the remaining amount in three months,” Raghav said.

The broker’s smile changed shape. “Sir, I told you on the phone. Another family has submitted the booking amount.”

“You also said they were considering it.”

“They confirmed this morning.”

“People should not be allowed to make major financial decisions before breakfast.”

The broker looked at his watch.

“All right,” Raghav said. “If they discover the tiles, call me.”

In the lift, he pressed the ground-floor button twice. It ignored him both times and stopped on the seventh floor to admit a woman holding a sleepy toddler.

The child stared at him.

Raghav crossed his eyes.

The child hid a smile against the woman’s shoulder.

By the time the doors opened in the lobby, Raghav was smiling too.

His phone rang as he walked towards the parking area.

“Kaisa laga?” his father asked.

“Bathroom tiles look like someone lost an argument with the colour brown.”

“And the flat?”

Raghav unlocked his car. “Booked.”

There was a pause. “By you?”

“By someone with faster banking reflexes.”

“I told you I could arrange some money.”

“And I told you to keep your retirement money where it is.”

“It is not useful sitting in an account.”

“It is extremely useful. It sits there and prevents me from developing blood pressure.”

His father sighed. “There will be another flat.”

“With better tiles.”

“Come for dinner.”

“Saturday.”

“You said that last Saturday.”

Raghav opened his mouth with a joke prepared. Something about being in high demand. Something easy.

“Tomorrow,” he said instead. “I’ll come tomorrow.”

“Good.”

The pleasure in his father’s voice was quiet enough to hurt.

After his mother left, it had been just the two of them for years. His father rarely asked twice for anything. Raghav had learned to fill the silence before either of them had to admit it was there.

Raghav ended the call and sat behind the wheel without starting the engine.

The balcony had not mattered. The apartment had not been unique. Delhi was full of buildings designed by people who believed beige was a personality.

Still, for a few minutes, he allowed himself to be disappointed.

Then he opened the group chat.

Raghav: Lost the flat.

Kabir replied first.

Kabir: Someone bought it?

Raghav: No. It escaped after seeing my finances.

Three dots appeared beside Samyukt’s name, disappeared and returned.

Samyukt: I’m sorry.

Raghav looked at the two words.

Raghav: Good. As punishment you are buying dinner.

Samyukt: Done.

Kabir: With what money?

Raghav: Excellent point. Kabir is buying dinner.

Kabir: I’m leaving this group.

Raghav started the car.

By ten thirty, he was in a conference room explaining to six senior managers why their software migration plan would fail.

He enjoyed this part of his job more than he admitted. A process map was a chessboard whose pieces complained in meetings. Every department protected its own square. Finance wanted fewer exceptions. Sales wanted every customer to be an exception. Technology wanted requirements that remained unchanged for more than forty-eight hours.

Raghav stood beside the screen and moved three tasks on the timeline.

“If training begins here,” he said, “operations will learn the new system before the final data fields are approved. Half the screenshots in the manual will be wrong by launch.”

The project manager frowned. “We cannot move the launch date.”

“I didn’t move it.”

“Then where do you recover the time?”

Raghav highlighted two approval stages. “These happen in sequence because the process document says they happen in sequence. The teams do not depend on each other. Run them together.”

“Compliance will object.”

“Compliance objected last week because nobody invited them until the end. Invite them now.”

“And if they reject the data structure?”

“Then we discover it on Thursday instead of next month.”

Silence settled over the table.

Raghav had learned that people often called a problem complicated when what they meant was that solving it required two departments to speak to each other.

The project manager studied the revised plan. “This might work.”

“That is the nicest thing anyone has said to me today.”

The meeting ended twelve minutes early. Two managers thanked him. One asked him to send the revised process map. The project manager presented the new schedule to the programme director that afternoon as something the team had developed together.

Raghav watched from the back of the room and made a note in the file where he kept ideas for his future company.

Credit should be assigned clearly. Good people leave when their work becomes ‘ours’ only after it succeeds.

Below it were notes on pricing, client discovery, recruitment and the kind of office he wanted. No grey cubicles. No motivational quotes written by people who underpaid their staff. A coffee machine capable of producing coffee rather than brown regret.

He had a name for the company too.

He had not told anyone. Saying it aloud would make it something that could fail.

At six, Raghav drove to Kabir’s office.

Kabir was waiting in the electronics lab with a circuit board in one hand and the expression of a man whose machine had betrayed him publicly. Rachna stood beside him, holding a tablet.

She wore her hair in a loose braid and had a small burn mark on the cuff of her shirt. Raghav had known her for almost two years and trusted her largely because she disagreed with Kabir without treating disagreement as a defect in his personality.

“He has killed it,” she said as Raghav entered.

Kabir did not look up. “It is not dead.”

On the workbench, a red indicator on a compact sensor unit blinked irregularly. Wires ran from it to a small electric motor.

“What was it before the tragedy?” Raghav asked.

Kabir adjusted a connector. “A vibration-monitoring module.”

“It listens to motors?”

“It measures vibration signatures and temperature drift. If the bearing starts failing, the pattern changes before the motor stops.”

Raghav leaned closer. “Predictive maintenance.”

“A cheaper version. The commercial units cost too much for small factories.”

Rachna handed him the tablet. A graph displayed several coloured lines and a sudden rise near the end.

“It detected the test fault,” she said. “Then it began producing this.”

The final section dissolved into dense, evenly spaced spikes.

Raghav pointed. “That looks familiar.”

Kabir finally glanced at him. “The seismic readings.”

For the past eight days, news channels had shown variations of the same pattern. A pulse without an earthquake. A vibration recorded across continents without a source anyone could locate.

“Interference?” Raghav asked.

“It shouldn’t be. The motor was off and the test rig was isolated. Local mechanical noise would not produce the same clean repetition across three runs.”

“Did you reproduce it?”

“Three times.”

Rachna folded her arms. “Which is why I told him to include it in tomorrow’s demonstration.”

Kabir shook his head. “I don’t know what caused it.”

“You can say that,” she replied.

“In front of the review panel?”

“Engineers are allowed to encounter things they don’t understand.”

“Not during funding reviews.”

Raghav scrolled through the results. The system was clever. More importantly, it solved a problem that cost small manufacturers real money.

“Why haven’t you shown this to your manager before?” he asked.

“It wasn’t ready.”

“What was missing?”

Kabir began listing problems. Battery consumption. False warnings. Sensor placement. Variations across different motors. Each concern was valid. Together, they built a wall tall enough to hide behind.

Raghav placed the tablet on the bench.

“A prototype’s job is to be ugly before people pay you to make it handsome.”

Rachna nodded. “I am stealing that.”

Kabir looked offended by both the metaphor and the alliance.

“Show the working detection,” Raghav continued. “Explain the limitations before they discover them. Keep the unexplained pulses in the appendix, because if you hide them and they appear during the demonstration, you will look dishonest instead of cautious.”

Kabir looked at the board again. “The enclosure is also temporary.”

“Then do not invite the panel to sit on it.”

Rachna laughed.

Kabir tried not to. His mouth failed first.

“You are both irritating,” he said.

“And yet essential,” Raghav replied.

They left the office twenty minutes later. Rachna had dinner plans with her sister, so Raghav and Kabir drove towards Samyukt’s shop.

The evening sky had the colour of unpolished steel. The air felt thick despite the traffic moving through it. Raghav lowered the car window and received heat, dust and three simultaneous horns.

He closed it again.

“Have you been sleeping properly?” Kabir asked.

“Define properly.”

“For enough hours.”

“Then no.”

“Headaches?”

Raghav glanced at him. “Are you selling medicine now?”

“Rachna has had a fever since yesterday. Two people in my office went home with body aches. I have had a headache for three days.”

Raghav flexed his fingers around the steering wheel. That morning, his hands had hurt as though he had spent the night gripping something. He had blamed the laptop, the drive, the apartment or age, in that order.

“Season change,” he said.

“It is September in Delhi. The season changes by becoming a different kind of hot.”

“Pollution.”

“Air quality is better than last week.”

“Work stress.”

“You finished a meeting early today. You sent a message to celebrate.”

Raghav looked at him. “Do you maintain a database of my excuses?”

“Someone has to.”

They found Samyukt at his desk with an untouched cup of tea and three calculators in front of him: a handheld one, one open on his computer and another on his phone. A stack of invoices sat beside him.

“Good,” Raghav said. “You have formed a calculator committee.”

Samyukt looked up. The shadows beneath his eyes had deepened since Monday. “One of them might give a different answer.”

“That is how accounting fraud begins.”

Kabir picked up the tea. “Cold.”

“Observant.”

“When did you eat?”

“I had lunch.”

“When?”

“During lunchtime.”

Raghav dropped into the chair opposite him. “He has become evasive. Interrogation begins now.”

They ordered rolls from the stall across the market. Samyukt protested about the price until Raghav reminded him that dinner had been imposed as punishment. When the food arrived, they ate at the desk among the invoices and chemical catalogues.

For ten minutes, conversation remained safely elsewhere. Kabir described his sensor. Raghav described the lost apartment as an architectural escape. Samyukt listened, laughed at the correct places and offered Kabir three useful questions about selling the device to small factories.

He was very good at solving problems that belonged to other people.

Raghav wiped his hands and placed the paper napkin beside the empty wrapper.

“Exactly how much do you owe?”

Samyukt’s expression did not change. Only his shoulders became still.

“We have discussed this.”

“We have discussed several smaller numbers that become a larger number when they meet in private.”

“I told you about Sethi.”

“You told us he wanted another payment. You did not tell us the hotel invoice had been delayed again.”

Samyukt looked at Kabir. “How do you know that?”

Kabir nodded towards the papers. The hotel’s name was printed across the top of the invoice.

“That could be anything,” Samyukt said.

“It says overdue,” Kabir replied.

“It could be an overdue invitation.”

Neither of them smiled.

Samyukt leaned back. “The payment will clear on Friday.”

“And Sethi?” Raghav asked.

“Handled.”

“That word has stopped meaning anything when you say it.”

Something flickered across Samyukt’s face. Anger, perhaps, or shame wearing anger’s clothes.

“What do you want me to do?” he asked. “Give you a daily report?”

“A truthful one would be a historic start.”

“Raghav,” Kabir said quietly.

The warning was deserved. Raghav heard the sharpness in his own voice and lowered it.

“I don’t want a report,” he said. “I want to know when my friend is drowning before he asks me to lend him a boat.”

“I asked you for money because there was no other option.”

“That is exactly the problem.”

Samyukt’s jaw tightened. “You want me to apologise for taking it?”

“No.”

Raghav answered too quickly, because that part was easy.

“I chose to help you. I would choose it again. But you call us when the problem needs money, then disappear when it needs people.”

The market noise moved beyond the glass door. A scooter passed. Someone argued over parking. Inside the shop, the ceiling fan clicked once on every rotation.

Kabir looked at Samyukt. “You spent years telling me that I could say what I was thinking without everyone judging me. Now you decide for us what we are allowed to know.”

Samyukt looked away.

“Maa and Ishita don’t know the full amount,” he said.

“We are not asking you to frighten them,” Kabir replied. “We are asking you to stop treating secrecy like protection.”

“If I tell everyone every time something goes wrong, what changes?”

“You stop being alone in the room,” Raghav said.

The answer escaped before he could make it sound lighter.

Samyukt looked at him then—not at the joke waiting to be made, not at the friend who always found a plan, but at the bare sentence underneath.

Raghav reached for the last roll.

“Also, someone stops you from making stupid decisions,” he said. “Shared responsibility. Very modern.”

The moment closed, but not completely.

Samyukt pushed the roll towards him. “Twelve.”

“Twelve what?”

“Lakh. Approximately. Four to you, three to Kabir, five connected to Sethi. The interest and payments make the last figure difficult to calculate.”

Raghav had known most of it. Hearing the total still changed the air.

Kabir folded his arms. “Does Sethi have anything in writing?”

“My father signed the original loan papers. I need to check what is enforceable now.”

“Good,” Raghav said. “We check. We map every payment, every document and every due date. Then we make a plan based on numbers instead of whichever creditor called last.”

Samyukt rubbed his forehead. “Can this wait until the hotel payment comes?”

“No.”

“Raghav—”

“You told us the number. That was the difficult part. Now let people be useful.”

Kabir opened his laptop without asking.

Samyukt stared at both of them, then gave a tired laugh. “You came here for free dinner and turned it into an audit.”

“Nothing in life is free,” Raghav said. “Especially Kabir’s dinner.”

The television mounted above the storage shelves changed from a cricket discussion to a live scientific briefing.

Behind the speaker stood a screen displaying the now-familiar seismic pattern.

“Increase the volume,” Kabir said.

Samyukt found the remote beneath a ledger.

“…confirmed that the recurring signals are increasing in frequency,” the scientist said. “Current models indicate that the pattern may reach an unknown critical threshold within three to four months. We emphasise that this does not constitute an earthquake prediction.”

A reporter raised her hand. “What happens at that threshold?”

“We do not yet know that a physical event will occur.”

“Then what exactly are you predicting?”

The scientist paused.

“The continuation of the observed trend.”

Raghav leaned back. “That is an expensive way of saying they don’t know.”

“They cannot invent an answer,” Kabir said.

“News channels do it every evening.”

The reporter asked whether the signals could be caused by secret weapons testing. Another asked about extraterrestrial activity. The scientist developed the expression of someone reconsidering every decision that had led her to a microphone.

Samyukt turned the volume down.

“Three months,” he said.

“Maybe four,” Raghav replied. “Plenty of time for the government to form a committee that forms another committee.”

Kabir was looking at his phone. “The newest interval was shorter than their published model predicted.”

“By how much?”

“I don’t know. The raw data isn’t updated.”

The shop lights dimmed.

All three men looked up.

The ceiling fan slowed, but the television remained on. A low vibration passed through the metal shutter. It was not loud enough to be called a sound. Raghav felt it in his molars and along the bones of his hands.

Outside, horns stopped one after another.

For three seconds, the market became unnaturally quiet.

Then every dog within hearing began to bark.

Samyukt gripped the edge of the desk. His face had lost colour.

“What happened?” Kabir asked.

“Heat,” Samyukt said through his teeth.

“Where?”

He pressed a palm beneath his ribs. “Here.”

The vibration deepened.

Raghav heard the market return all at once: voices, feet, engines, a child crying near the food stall, someone shouting for an electrician. Within the noise, individual voices separated with impossible clarity.

Move.

Wait.

Where are you?

The words had been spoken somewhere in the market, but he knew the direction of each voice before he found its speaker. For one impossible moment, the people around him felt as clearly positioned as pieces arranged across a chessboard.

Then the lights brightened and the sensation vanished.

The fan resumed its clicking rotation.

Kabir had gone to the front of the shop. “No earthquake.”

The hanging sample bottles were still. Nothing on the shelves had moved.

Samyukt took a slow breath. Colour returned gradually to his face.

“We should go,” Kabir said.

They closed the shop together. Samyukt insisted he was well enough to ride home. Kabir inspected him with open disbelief, but after ten minutes the heat had passed and Samyukt refused every alternative.

“Message when you reach,” Raghav told him.

“Yes, Maa.”

“Maa would make you leave the bike here.”

Samyukt put on his helmet. “Which is why we are not telling her.”

“Excellent. Our first lesson about openness has transformed him.”

Samyukt raised one hand and rode away.

Raghav and Kabir walked towards the parking area. The market had recovered with remarkable speed. Shopkeepers blamed the power grid. Customers resumed bargaining. A man outside the pharmacy replayed a video of the lights dimming, although the recording showed almost nothing.

Kabir carried the vibration module in his backpack. As they reached Raghav’s car, Kabir stopped and opened the backpack’s front pocket.

“The board is active,” he said.

“You left it on?”

“There is no battery.”

He pulled the module out. A red indicator glowed faintly beneath the translucent casing.

Raghav leaned closer.

The light went dark.

Kabir opened the battery compartment. Empty.

Neither man spoke for several seconds.

“Stored charge?” Raghav suggested.

“Not for this long.”

“Faulty capacitor?”

“Maybe.”

Kabir did not sound convinced.

He placed the device carefully inside his bag. Raghav unlocked the car and walked around to the driver’s side.

Raghav.

He turned.

Kabir stood near the bonnet, looking down at his phone. His mouth had not moved.

“What?” Raghav asked.

Kabir looked up. “I didn’t say anything.”

Raghav studied his face. “You called me.”

“No.”

The pressure in the air returned for a single heartbeat.

Kabir’s expression changed.

“Raghav,” he said in the same tone Raghav had heard a moment earlier. “Something is wrong.”
