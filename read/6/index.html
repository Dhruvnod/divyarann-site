Chapter Six

Jagaran

जागरण

The first thing Anantadarshi knew was that the silence had changed.

For longer than he could have measured, the silence inside him had been complete. Breath had slowed until it was a rumour. The heart had kept a rhythm so patient that whole seasons passed between beats. Thought had folded itself away like a cloth that would not be needed. What remained was a single thread of attention, held so lightly that it did not disturb the thing it attended to: the current that ran beneath all things, steady as a river under ice.

Now the river was rising.

He felt it before he understood it. The current pressed against the sealed vessel of his body the way floodwater presses against a door. It came in tides, each heavier than the last, and between the tides it withdrew with a long dragging pull, as though the world itself were breathing badly.

The thread of attention tightened. That was the first mistake, and he knew it as he made it. Attention became thought. Thought became a question. And a question, after so long, was a stone dropped into still water.

The seal broke.

Pain arrived all at once, from everywhere. His lungs tore themselves open on air that tasted of dust and iron. His heart, startled, struck his ribs like a fist on a locked door. Every joint he possessed informed him that it had not been consulted about this and did not consent.

He fell forward onto stone.

For some time he lay there and did the only thing he still remembered how to do. He breathed. In. Out. The breath was ragged and shallow and it hurt, but it obeyed him, and so he gave it his whole mind until it steadied.

Then he opened his eyes.

Darkness. Not the darkness behind closed lids, which is warm and belongs to oneself, but the cold outer darkness of a place where no lamp had burned in a very long time. He lay in it and let his eyes do what they could. Slowly, a shape emerged: a grey smear of light, low and to his left, where the mouth of the cave had been.

Had been. He corrected himself. Still was. He was the one who had been elsewhere.

He tried to rise and discovered that his body had opinions about this too. His arms shook. His legs were cords of pain with no strength in them. When he finally sat upright, the effort left him breathless and sweating, and the sweat alarmed him more than the pain. Sweat meant the body was spending water it did not have.

There had been a seep at the back of the cave, long ago, where the stone wept into a hollow. He turned his head towards it, and the small movement set the whole chamber turning. He waited for the turning to stop. Then he began to crawl.

The water was still there. He drank it in sips, the way one feeds a sick child, and felt it go down into him like news from a far country.

Afterwards, with his back against the wall, he let his attention go outward.

This was the discipline that had carried him into the long silence, and it had not left him. The body was weak, but the subtle sense was not, and what it found made him forget the body entirely.

The current was wrong.

Not corrupt. Not yet. But swollen, thick, unripe, like fruit forced on a tree too early. It filled the air of the chamber the way smoke fills a room. It filled the ground beneath him. And it was not resting. It was being pulled, gently and continuously, in a direction he could feel but not see, as every stream on a mountain is pulled towards the one valley.

He followed the pull with his mind.

It gathered. Somewhere not far from this place, perhaps a day’s walking for a man who could walk, it was gathering into a knot so dense that his attention slid off its surface. And beyond it, at distances his sense could barely hold, there were others. He counted them, the near one among them, as a blind man counts the pillars of a hall by the echo of his own voice.

Seven.

He did not know what a knot like that would do when it tightened. He knew only that nothing he had learned in all his years before the silence described it, and that the tides which had woken him were its heartbeat.

He said the one name that had never failed to steady him, and said it badly, with a voice that cracked on the second syllable. “Narayana.”

The cave did not answer. It never had. That was not what the name was for.

It took him three days to reach the cave mouth. He measured them by the grey light coming and going. He spent them learning to stand, then learning to stand without the wall, then learning to take a step and remain upright at the end of it. He ate nothing because there was nothing, and he found, with the detachment of a man observing someone else, that hunger was slower to return than pain.

On the third evening he stood in the opening and looked out at the world.

The forest was gone.

He had entered the silence in a wood so old that its trees had names. He had expected, in whatever part of him had expected anything, to find it changed. He had not expected to find it absent. The slope below the cave fell away in scrub and broken stone, and beyond it the land was flat and bare and crossed by hard pale lines that caught the last light like water but did not move like water.

And beyond that, where the sky should have darkened, it glowed.

The glow was the colour of embers and it lay along the whole southern horizon, so wide that he could not see its ends. Within it, points of harder light stood in rows. Some of them blinked. One of them was moving: a star that travelled in a straight line, low and slow, and did not fall.

Something came from the glow that was not quite a sound. It was like a river, but no river ran so evenly, and no river ran without pausing to breathe.

He stood for a long time and tried to make the thing in front of him into something he knew. He could not. Whatever it was, the knot was above it. He could feel it hanging there, a weight pressing on the glow like a thumb on a bruise.

So that was where he would go.

He took the first step down the slope, and the ground told him, through the soles of his feet, that another tide was coming in.

On Friday, the government stopped using the word anomaly.

Samyukt watched the briefing on the shop television with a bulk order of floor cleaner half-packed behind him. The scientist from the earlier broadcasts had been replaced by a man in a suit, which Samyukt had learned was never a good sign. Behind the suit hung a world map with seven soft-edged shapes on it, like bruises. The largest of the shapes lay across northern India.

“Based on the revised analysis,” the suit said, “the activity is expected to reach its critical threshold within a window of eleven to sixteen days. The public is advised to review earthquake preparedness guidelines, which are available on the ministry’s website.”

“What is at the threshold?” a reporter asked.

“We are prepared for a significant seismic event.”

“Is it a seismic event?”

The suit consulted his papers. “We are prepared for one.”

“Last week this ministry said three to four months.”

“The analysis has been revised.”

By noon the market had a different sound. Thursday’s pulse had already put glass on the pavements in three sectors; the briefing put a number under it. It was not panic. Dwarka did not panic; it queued. There was a queue at the pharmacy, a queue at the petrol pump that curled around the corner and into the service lane, and a queue at the ATM that nobody in it could explain, since the banks had announced nothing. Men on scooters carried water cans between their knees. A man outside the hardware shop was selling torches from a cardboard box and explaining to every customer that he was not raising prices, which meant he had raised them.

The pulses themselves had become furniture. They came every few minutes now, a stutter in the fan and a shiver in the sample bottles that nobody looked up for any more.

At one fifteen, Samyukt’s phone showed a bank notification he had stopped expecting.

The hotel had paid.

He read the amount three times. Then he sat down on a drum of surfactant and laughed, once, in a way that made the packer look up.

The payment was six weeks late. The money had arrived in the one week when nobody in the city wanted anything but rice and candles, and thirty-five thousand of it was already spoken for: the figure Sethi had rolled from Saturday to Saturday for six weeks while the hotel did the same to Samyukt.

He opened the group chat.

Samyukt: Hotel paid.

Raghav: Finally. The system works. Slowly, like a glacier, but it works.

Kabir: Sethi?

Samyukt: 35 due Saturday. Asking before I pay. As agreed.

Raghav: Look at him. Growth.

Kabir: Pay him. Then buy rice.

Raghav: Then buy rice. Approved by committee.

He transferred the money that afternoon. It did not feel like a victory. It felt like setting down one bag so he could pick up another.

Mr Sethi came anyway on Saturday, which Samyukt had not expected. He had assumed that the transfer would make the visit unnecessary. Instead, the man stood in the doorway in his pale grey shirt and looked at the shelves of disinfectant as though he had never seen them before.

“You received it?” Samyukt asked.

“Yes.” Mr Sethi did not sit. “I came to say that the schedule we discussed can wait.”

Samyukt waited for the condition.

There was none.

“My daughter is in Gurgaon,” Mr Sethi said. “Her building is twenty-two floors. She says it has been certified.” He said the word the way other people said haunted. “Where will you take Naina ji?”

“We’re staying.”

Mr Sethi nodded slowly, as if this confirmed something he had long suspected about Samyukt, and left without mentioning the loan again. At the door he paused beside Samar’s photograph, as he always did, and this time he said nothing at all.

The heat beneath Samyukt’s ribs no longer came and went. Sometime in the past week it had simply stayed, a low steady warmth he had stopped noticing, the way one stops noticing a clock. The fever was gone. The body pain was gone. What remained was an alertness he did not trust, as though he had drunk too much tea and the feeling had decided to become permanent.

On Sunday he went to the range before opening the shop. He had been going most mornings since the first time; it was the only hour of the day when the alertness had somewhere to go. The target with his buried arrow had been retired to a corner of the equipment shed, where it stood against the wall with the shaft still in it like an accusation. The instructor had stopped asking about it. He had also stopped standing behind Samyukt to watch him shoot, which was its own kind of comment.

Six ends. Thirty-six arrows. Thirty-one in the gold.

Samyukt looked at the grouping and felt no pride at all. Pride would have required him to believe it was his.

On Monday he tried to send his mother and sister away.

He had planned it carefully. Naina’s cousin in Alwar had a house with a courtyard and no building taller than two floors within a kilometre. The trains were still running. He would stay to manage the shop and join them if things became serious, and he said all of this at dinner in the voice he used with suppliers.

Naina let him finish.

“No,” she said.

“Maa—”

“Your father sent me to my mother’s house after the blasts in 2005. Did you know that?” She served him dal without looking up. “I spent eleven days in Bikaner with you holding my hand and your sister in a basket, listening to the radio and imagining him dead. He was fine. He was still selling from a rented godown then, before there was a board to paint his name on, and he sold more bleach that month than in any other month that year. When I came back, I told him that if he ever sent me away again, I would not come back at all.”

Ishita, who had never heard this story, put her spoon down.

“I am not going to Alwar,” Naina said. “If the earth is going to shake, it can shake with all three of us in the same room.”

Samyukt opened his mouth to argue and discovered that the thing in his chest was not frustration.

It was relief.

“Fine,” he said.

“Fine,” said Ishita. “Also, my entrance exam is in two weeks. I am not sitting it in Alwar.”

“That is after the window,” Samyukt said.

“Then it will be after the window. Pass the salad.”

On Tuesday night, there was no game. Raghav came over instead, with Kabir and a sheet of paper that was not a spreadsheet, which Samyukt found more alarming than any spreadsheet.

“Meeting point,” Raghav said, spreading it on the dining table between the water glasses. He had drawn Sector 12 from memory: the shop, the flat, Kabir’s office, his own office and the metro station circled twice. “If phones go, we don’t wander around looking for each other. We go here. The concourse, near the ticket counters. Whoever arrives first waits.”

“Why the metro?” Ishita asked.

“Concrete pillars this thick.” He held his hands apart. “An open concourse, fewer nearby façades, and everyone knows where it is. It isn’t safe. It’s the least bad place all of us can reach.”

Naina looked at the sheet for a long moment. “You have thought about this.”

“I think about everything, Aunty. It’s a medical condition.”

Kabir had said very little. He sat with his backpack on his knees, and when Samyukt looked at him, he unzipped the front pocket without being asked.

The vibration module lay inside. Its indicator glowed a steady, patient red.

“Battery?” Samyukt asked.

“Still none.” Kabir zipped the pocket closed. “It hasn’t gone dark since Thursday’s pulse.”

“And the review?”

“The second one is postponed. Everything is postponed.” A pause. “Rachna has a fever again.”

Raghav, who had been drawing an arrow from the shop to the station, stopped drawing. “You didn’t say.”

“I’m saying.”

Nobody made a joke. Samyukt noticed Raghav glance repeatedly towards the balcony door, where the sounds of the street came in, as though he were listening for something underneath them.

“Hearing them again?” Samyukt asked quietly.

Raghav shrugged. “Only in crowds. I’ve started taking the stairs instead of the lift.”

On Wednesday, the window moved.

It moved the way the hotel’s payment date had always moved: quietly, without anyone saying so aloud. The estimate did more than count down; it narrowed. Eleven to sixteen days became six to nine. Then, on the evening bulletin, the ministry’s spokesman used the phrase in the coming days and did not give a number at all.

Samyukt closed the shop at seven. He rode home through streets where every third shop had its shutter half down and the people inside were taping the glass.

On Wednesday, Kairavi’s father came home at six, which had happened once before, last week, and never in the years before that.

“There is nothing left to sell,” Rajeev said, and sat down at the dining table without taking off his shoes. “Rice, dal, oil, water, candles, batteries. Gone. A woman bought forty packets of biscuits. Forty. I asked her what she was going to do with forty packets of biscuits and she started crying, so I gave her a discount.”

Meera put a cup of tea in front of him. He looked at it as if it were a document requiring his signature.

The office had been closed for inspection since Thursday. Kairavi worked at the dining table now, with her laptop angled away from the window because the glass unsettled her. The pulses came every few minutes. The glass no longer trembled; it hummed, faintly, a note just below hearing that she felt in her back teeth.

Twice that week she had seen the lines again.

The first time, a pulse had passed while she was washing a cup, and the window frame had lit from within: thin bright threads along the aluminium, a knot of them at the corner where the frame met the wall, fading before she could decide whether they were there. The second time she had been ready. She had kept her eyes on the frame through the whole pulse, and the lines had come and gone in under a second, and this time she had written down what she saw before she wrote down what she thought about it.

Frame, top left: three lines converge. Same corner both times. Corner has a hairline crack in the paint. Lines appear only during pulse. Not visible to Aarohi (asked).

It was not an explanation. It was data. She had decided she could live with that.

“We should decide where we meet,” she said. “If the phones go.”

Rajeev looked up from his tea.

She had expected him to resist, or to hand down a decision. Instead, he offered one. “The metro station. Sector 12. It is between the supermarket and the house, and all four of us know how to reach it without a phone.”

“That was my thought too.”

“I asked the station manager about the concourse.” He drank his tea. “He said it is open and the pillars are reinforced. He also said the station will fill quickly, so we should reach it before the crowd does.”

Kairavi looked at her father for a moment longer than the sentence required.

“All right,” she said. “The metro.”

She had not argued. She noticed it, and saw Rajeev notice it too, and neither of them said anything about it. It was the second time in a week they had agreed without a fight, and it had taken the end of the world to arrange.

Aarohi’s sheet had grown to two hundred rows. She had stopped collecting videos of trembling windows and started collecting hospital notices instead: which emergency rooms were full, which had moved patients to lower floors, which had asked staff to stay. She read the pattern out loud to nobody in particular.

“Everyone with fever is being sent home. They’re keeping beds.”

“For what?” Kairavi asked.

“They don’t say.”

Meera had packed the documents into a biscuit tin. Passports, Aadhaar cards, the house papers, the photograph of her own mother she had carried through three moves. The tin sat on the shoe rack by the door with a torch on top of it, and every time Kairavi passed it she felt the same two things at once: that it was absurd, and that it was the most sensible object in the flat.

Dr Menon called at ten past eleven.

“I will keep this short,” she said. “We are briefing the cabinet at six tomorrow morning. The number we are giving them is two days.”

Kairavi closed her bedroom door. “Two.”

“Possibly less. The signals under your region are converging faster than under any other. We no longer have a model that fits; we have a line and we are following it down.” A pause, filled with the sound of other people’s keyboards. “I wanted you to hear it from me before you heard it from a man in a suit.”

“Thank you.”

“Don’t thank me. Stay away from glass. Keep your family close.” Another pause, shorter. “You were right about the acceleration, Kairavi. I’m sorry we were slow.”

“You were careful.”

“That is a kinder word for it than I deserve.”

When the call ended, Kairavi sat on the edge of her bed for a while with the phone in her lap. Then she went out to the dining table, where her father was still sitting in his shoes, and her mother was pretending to watch television, and her sister had fallen asleep with her head on her laptop.

She told them.

Aarohi woke up halfway through. Meera’s hand found the edge of the table. Rajeev did not move at all, and when Kairavi had finished, he asked the only question that mattered.

“What happens?”

“I don’t know what happens,” Kairavi said. “I know when.”

Thursday began as the other days had begun, which was the strangest thing about it.

Samyukt opened the shop at eight. The market was quieter than usual and the queues had thinned, not because people had stopped being afraid but because there was nothing left to queue for. The pharmacy’s shutter was down with a handwritten sign: NO STOCK. GOD BLESS. The momo stall was open. The man who ran it had told Samyukt on Wednesday that he had survived two earthquakes in Nepal and did not intend to be frightened by a graph.

The heat beneath his ribs was higher than it had been. He kept a hand pressed to the spot when nobody was looking.

At ten forty, a housekeeping contractor arrived with a tempo and an order for sixty cans of disinfectant, and the morning became ordinary in the way that only work can make a morning ordinary. Samyukt counted cans. The packer loaded. The contractor stood in the doorway talking about contracts for afterwards, as though afterwards were a place you could drive to.

At eleven oh six, the ceiling fan slowed.

It did not stop. It slowed, as it had slowed the night the shop lights dimmed with Raghav and Kabir at the desk, and Samyukt’s eyes went to it before his mind did. The contractor stopped talking. Out in the market, the horns stopped one by one, not together, like a conversation dying.

Then the air changed.

The air in the shop, which a moment ago had been air, became heavy. It pressed on his ears. It pressed on his chest from the outside, in the same place the heat pressed from the inside, and for one disorienting second the two pressures met and he could not breathe at all.

The sample bottles on their hooks swung away from the door, deeper into the shop.

Not towards the glass. Away from it. As though something outside had exhaled against the building.

Every pigeon in the market went up at once. Samyukt heard them, a single tearing sound of wings, and then he heard nothing, because the sound had gone. All of it. The fan, the tempo’s engine, the contractor’s breathing, his own heartbeat. The world went silent the way a television goes silent when the cable is pulled, and in that silence, every phone in the shop lit up with the same screen.

He did not read the rest.

The glass door bowed inward. He saw it happen: the pane curving in its frame like the skin of a drum, a long slow flex that should have shattered it and did not. The contractor said something. Samyukt could see his mouth move. Samyukt walked past him, into the doorway, and looked up.

Later, he would not be able to say what he had expected. Clouds, perhaps. A tremor. The things the posters had prepared him for.

The sky over Dwarka was folding.

There was no other way to describe it. The blue above the apartment blocks had creased along a line he could not see, and the two halves of it were drawing towards each other as the pages of a book draw towards the spine. Light bent along the crease. Sound returned all at once, every horn and voice and dog in the market, and under them a note so low he felt it in the ground before he heard it, a single sustained beat like the first stroke of a drum the size of the city.

The crease split.

It tore from one end of the sky to the other, and what lay behind it was not sky. It was dark, and it moved, and it was close. Far closer than clouds. Close enough that he could see the edges of the tear ripple, as though whatever was behind it were pressing to get through.

The heat under his ribs rose into his throat.

The contractor was screaming now. People were running in both directions along the market, which meant that nobody knew which direction was away. Samyukt’s phone buzzed against his leg. He did not look at it. He could not look at anything but the tear, which was widening, and the darkness behind it, which had stopped moving like weather and started moving like something that had seen him.

Something came through.

It fell the way nothing alive should fall, limbs loose, without fear, as if falling were simply the shortest route to the ground. It struck the roof of a building three streets away. The roof gave. The building shook. Dust rose in a slow brown column.

And in the dust, the thing that had fallen stood up.
