Chapter Four

Aahvan

आह्वान
the summons
Samyukt · about 15 minutes

The doctor told Samyukt to rest as if she had prescribed a rare medicine available only in another country.

“How many hours do you sleep?” she asked.

Samyukt considered lying. Naina was sitting beside him, which made the attempt pointless.

“Six,” he said.

“Four,” Naina corrected.

“Sometimes six.”

“When?”

“On special occasions.”

The doctor did not smile. She turned back to the report on her screen.

The clinic smelled of antiseptic and the eucalyptus oil someone in the waiting room had applied generously enough to treat the entire building. A faded poster behind the desk explained the symptoms of seasonal influenza. Samyukt had mentally checked off fever, fatigue and body pain, then rejected the diagnosis because the poster looked too cheerful.

For twelve days, his temperature had risen and fallen without settling into a pattern. The ache in his hands had spread to his shoulders and back. Sleep came in short, restless pieces. Sometimes he woke with the certainty that a loud sound had crossed the room, only to find the house silent.

Every test the doctor had ordered was normal.

“Your blood count is fine,” she said. “There is no obvious infection. It may be a mild viral illness combined with exhaustion and dehydration.”

“So I’m healthy?”

“That is not what I said.”

“But it is close.”

Naina looked at the doctor. “He does this.”

“I can see that.”

The doctor added a note to the prescription. “Fluids, regular meals and proper sleep. No strenuous activity for a few days. If the fever rises, the pain worsens or you develop breathing difficulty, come back immediately.”

“How many days should he stay home?” Naina asked.

“At least two.”

Samyukt sat straighter. “The reports are normal.”

“Your reports do not run your business,” Naina said.

“Exactly.”

“That was not support.”

The doctor placed the prescription between them. “Two days.”

They left the clinic at eleven fifteen. At eleven twenty-three, Samyukt told Naina he needed to stop at the shop for ten minutes.

At eleven forty, she stood inside his office with her arms folded while he answered a supplier’s call.

“I am leaving,” she announced when he finished.

“I’ll come home after checking the dispatch.”

“Ten minutes ended.”

“Business time moves differently.”

“Then your business can explain that to the doctor.”

She took the motorcycle keys from his desk.

“Maa.”

“Call a cab when you are finished being foolish.”

She left with the keys.

Samyukt watched her through the glass door. One of the workers near the packing table suddenly became absorbed in aligning labels.

“Not a word,” Samyukt said.

The man nodded wisely.

Samyukt stayed for the rest of the afternoon.

The unexplained seismic pulses had become part of ordinary conversation. Customers discussed them while arguing over prices. Delivery drivers forwarded videos of shaking windows and barking dogs. News channels placed countdown graphics beside experts who repeatedly explained that there was no official countdown.

The government had formed an international research group. Universities had opened their data to one another. Monitoring stations were being recalibrated and new sensors installed near major cities. The official projection remained unchanged: if the trend continued, the unknown threshold might be reached in three to four months.

Meanwhile, clinics across several countries were reporting unexplained fevers, muscle pain and disturbed sleep.

Most doctors blamed anxiety, weather or the tendency of social media to make unrelated symptoms appear connected. The health ministry had found no common pathogen. There was no reason for panic.

The phrase appeared so often that people had begun printing it on T-shirts.

At two, Raghav and Kabir arrived carrying a laptop and enough determination to qualify as an enforcement agency.

“Why are you here?” Samyukt asked.

Raghav looked around. “Interesting. I was about to ask the patient the same question.”

“Maa called you?”

“She sent a voice note.”

“How long?”

“One minute and forty-eight seconds.”

Samyukt winced. “That bad?”

“She used my full name.”

Kabir moved the invoices off the spare chair and opened his laptop. “Sit.”

“I am sitting.”

“Continue.”

For the next hour, they worked through the debt.

Raghav had built a spreadsheet with coloured categories, payment priorities and three possible cash-flow plans. Kabir had scanned the original loan documents and arranged every transfer Samyukt could find in date order. The oldest receipts were in Samar’s handwriting. Several payments had been recorded only as interest, and two handwritten additions to the schedule had no signature from Samar at all.

“This is not a legal opinion,” Kabir said, “but you should show it to someone who can give one.”

“A lawyer will cost money.”

“Less than paying an invalid charge for another five years,” Raghav replied.

Samyukt looked at the total projected interest and felt the familiar pressure gather behind his eyes.

“Sethi will not like this.”

“We are building a financial plan,” Raghav said. “His happiness is not a column.”

“It should be,” Kabir said. “Set the value to zero.”

Samyukt laughed despite the headache.

The sound felt unfamiliar inside the office.

Raghav turned the laptop towards him. “Nothing gets paid outside this order unless all three of us discuss it.”

“It is my debt.”

“Correct.”

“Then why do you get a vote?”

“Because seven lakh of your debt is sitting in these two handsome chairs.”

Kabir glanced at the cracked plastic beneath him. “Speak for yourself.”

Samyukt looked at the plan again.

His first instinct was to find the flaw, thank them and return to handling it himself. The plan could fail. The hotel could delay payment again. A customer could cancel. Raw-material prices could rise. Depending on another person created one more place where something could go wrong.

It also created one more place where something might hold.

“Fine,” he said. “We discuss it.”

Raghav extended his hand across the desk.

“This is not a business merger,” Samyukt said.

“It could be. Your company has chemicals. Mine will have solutions. Kabir has one blinking box.”

“The box works,” Kabir said.

Samyukt shook Raghav’s hand.

The heat beneath his ribs stirred.

He released the grip and pressed his palm against his abdomen.

Kabir noticed. “Again?”

“Acidity.”

“Your acidity reacts to seismic activity?”

“It is very informed.”

Neither friend accepted the answer, but a delivery tempo arrived before they could continue.

That evening, Samyukt returned home in a cab. Naina did not say she had been right. She did not need to. She placed khichdi in front of him and watched him eat.

Ishita had taken over the living-room floor. Books, old files and cardboard boxes surrounded her in uneven territories.

“What happened?” Samyukt asked.

“Maa said the cupboard smells.”

“The cupboard is older than you. Show respect.”

“The cupboard is a biological event.”

She reached into a long canvas case and lifted out what lay inside.

Samyukt stopped eating.

The recurve bow had once been black. Years of use had left pale marks near the grip, and dust had settled into the shallow scratches along its limbs. The string had been removed and coiled in a separate pouch. His arm guard, finger tab, old scorecards and a few medals lay beneath it.

“I thought you sold this,” Ishita said.

“Why would I sell it?”

“Because you haven’t touched it in three years.”

He set his spoon down and took the bow from her.

The weight returned to his hand with unsettling familiarity. His fingers found the grip before he looked at it. For a moment, he was twelve again, standing at the edge of a training range with a borrowed bow too heavy for him and the image of Karna’s chariot fixed inside his head.

He had first discovered the Mahabharata through television, then through every version he could find. Karna had fascinated him: the abandoned child who became a king, the warrior denied recognition, the giver who refused to stop giving even when generosity stripped away his protection.

At twelve, Samyukt had seen only greatness in that endurance.

At twenty-five, he was less certain where greatness ended and tragedy began.

Ishita wiped dust from one of the medals. “You were good.”

“I am still good.”

“Of course. That’s why the bow lives with expired warranties.”

“Archery does not expire.”

“People get rusty.”

“Metal gets rusty.”

“You manufacture chemicals. Fix yourself.”

Naina sat opposite him. “Go on Sunday.”

“Where?”

“The range.”

“The doctor said no strenuous activity.”

Ishita stared at him. “You ignored the doctor for work, but suddenly her word is sacred when someone suggests enjoyment?”

“Archery is not enjoyment. It is discipline.”

“Even better. You desperately need some.”

Samyukt examined the old string. It would need replacing. The leather on the finger tab had stiffened. Everything could be restored.

That thought produced a feeling he did not trust.

Hope, perhaps. Or grief with better posture.

“Why did you stop?” Ishita asked.

“Work.”

“You worked before you were twenty-two.”

“Not like this.”

“So you stopped making time.”

“There wasn’t time to make.”

Ishita put the medal down. “There is always time for things everyone else needs from you.”

Samyukt looked at her.

She was not accusing him. That made the sentence harder to resist.

“Sunday,” she said. “I’m coming with you.”

“Why?”

“To protect innocent targets.”

On Sunday morning, the city woke beneath a colourless sky.

The archery range occupied a large sports ground between a school and an unfinished housing project. Samyukt had trained there for years, although the entrance had been repainted and the equipment shed replaced since his last visit. The grass was damp from overnight watering. Targets stood at measured distances, their bright circles almost rude against the grey morning.

The instructor on duty inspected Samyukt’s bow and refused to let him use the old string.

“Limbs are fine,” he said. “String is not. Use this for today.”

He handed over a replacement and watched Samyukt string the bow.

The motion pulled sharply across Samyukt’s shoulders.

He hid the discomfort from Ishita, who was sitting behind the safety line with two cups of tea and the alertness of a person waiting for public failure.

Samyukt fastened his arm guard. The familiar sequence returned in pieces: stance, grip, nock, hook, raise, draw.

His first arrow landed outside the blue ring.

Ishita lowered her tea. “Still good?”

“Wind.”

The flags above the targets hung motionless.

“Very selective wind.”

The second arrow struck low. The third landed left.

Samyukt felt irritation rise with every shot. His mind knew where his elbow belonged, how the string should settle against his face and how the release should happen without being forced. His body delivered approximations.

By the sixth arrow, his shoulder trembled.

He lowered the bow.

The fever had gone that morning, but the ache remained deep inside his joints. He had expected the discipline to return like a switch being turned on. Instead, every neglected year stood with him at the shooting line.

“Take a break,” Ishita called.

“I just started.”

“Your arm disagrees.”

Samyukt stepped away from the line and drank water. Around him, younger archers released in steady rhythm. Strings snapped. Arrows struck foam. An instructor corrected someone’s foot position with the patience of a person repeating the same sentence for the thousandth time.

Ishita handed him the tea.

“Karna would have hit the centre,” she said.

“Karna did not take a three-year break.”

“He also had divine armour. You have a medical prescription.”

Samyukt smiled into the cup.

Ishita turned one of his old medals over in her hand. She had brought it without telling him.

“You know what is strange?” she asked. “When you talk about Karna, you never talk about whether he was happy.”

“That is not why people remember him.”

“Exactly.”

Samyukt looked towards the targets.

“He remained generous after people humiliated him,” he said. “He kept his word when breaking it would have saved him. He became great without being given the opportunities other people had.”

“I understand why you admire him.” Ishita traced the edge of the medal with her thumb. “I just think you admire what he endured so much that sometimes you confuse enduring everything with doing the right thing.”

The words settled more deeply than Samyukt wanted them to.

“When did a BA in psychology make you an expert on the Mahabharata?”

“It didn’t. Growing up with you made me an expert on you.”

The instructor called the next round.

Samyukt returned to the line.

This time, he stopped trying to prove that nothing had changed.

He widened his stance by half an inch, eased the tension in his bow hand and accepted the tremor in his shoulder. He breathed into his abdomen, slowly, until the noise around him separated from the work in front of him.

Raise.

Draw.

Anchor.

Release.

The arrow landed in the red.

The next joined it. The third drifted into blue, but the grouping had tightened.

Samyukt continued.

The old satisfaction returned without drama. It was not the pleasure of hitting the centre. It was the relief of discovering that improvement still obeyed effort.

On his final end, he raised the bow and felt heat gather beneath his ribs.

He almost lowered it.

Instead, he held.

The warmth rose through his chest and divided across his shoulders. It travelled down both arms—not painfully, but with the smooth pressure of water finding an old channel.

His fingers stopped trembling.

The bow became light in his hand.

For one breath, the target appeared impossibly close. Samyukt could see a torn fibre beside the yellow circle, the shadow cast by the previous arrow and the exact movement of the paper face in air currents he could not feel.

He released.

The bowstring snapped forward with a sound deeper than it should have made.

The arrow hit near the centre and disappeared into the target up to its fletching. Whatever had moved through him had found an old path, flared once and vanished before he could understand it. When he reached for that impossible steadiness again, he found only the ordinary ache in his shoulders.

Everyone on the line stopped.

Samyukt lowered the bow.

The heat vanished. Pain rushed back into his shoulder.

The instructor walked downrange after the signal and examined the target. When he pulled, the arrow refused to move.

“What poundage is this?” he called.

Samyukt told him.

The instructor looked at the buried shaft, then back at the bow.

“That is not possible.”

Ishita had stopped smiling.

Samyukt flexed his fingers. They felt warm, but otherwise ordinary.

“Damaged foam,” he said.

The instructor moved to the rear of the target and inspected it again.

“Maybe,” he replied, without conviction.

On the ride home, Ishita asked about the shot twice. Samyukt blamed the target twice. The third time, she remained quiet.

By evening, his fever had returned.

He ate dinner in bed under Naina’s supervision and fell asleep before ten. The old bow rested inside its canvas case near the cupboard. His final arrow remained at the range because removing it would have damaged both the shaft and the target.

At some point during the night, the ceiling fan disappeared.

Samyukt stood barefoot on a plain covered in ash.

There was no horizon. Grey earth met a black sky without distance between them. Wind moved across the ground in long, pale sheets, lifting ash without touching his clothes.

Above him, the sky was fractured.

The break ran from one end of darkness to the other—a thin wound with no stars behind it. Something pressed against the far side. Not a body. Not a face. A weight too large for either.

A beat travelled through the ground.

Samyukt felt it in his teeth.

Another followed.

It was the same pulse the instruments had recorded, slowed until the space between one vibration and the next became enormous.

The third beat carried another sound within it.

A damaru.

The rhythm crossed the plain. With every beat, the ash lifted higher.

Far ahead, someone sat in meditation.

Samyukt could not judge the distance. The figure seemed both beyond reach and close enough to touch. Matted hair rose above his head. A crescent moon shone against it without illuminating the darkness. Ash covered his skin. At his throat rested a blue so deep it seemed to contain its own night.

A trident stood in the ground beside him.

Samyukt knew.

His knees weakened. He lowered his head and brought his palms together, but no prayer came. Every mantra he had read, every name he knew and every question he had ever imagined vanished beneath the fact of that presence.

Between them, something emerged from the ash.

A bow.

It was taller than the one Samyukt had used that morning. Its limbs curved like two fragments of darkness drawn into shape. There was no string. Beneath the black surface, thin veins of violet light moved and disappeared.

Samyukt approached it.

The sky cracked wider.

From beyond the opening came a distant mass of voices. Screams, prayers, commands and names called by people who expected an answer.

He reached for the bow.

The grip was cold.

Samyukt pulled.

It did not move.

He tightened both hands and planted his feet. The ash gave way beneath them. The bow remained fixed in the earth.

The meditating figure opened his eyes.

The plain became still.

The question reached Samyukt without crossing the distance between them.

“What are you willing to destroy in order to protect what you love?”

Samyukt looked towards the broken sky. The voices beyond it grew louder.

He thought of Naina sitting beside him at the clinic. Ishita holding his forgotten medal. Raghav hiding disappointment inside jokes. Kabir opening the loan documents without judgement. His father struggling for breath beside him in the car.

His hands closed around the bow.

“Whatever tries to harm them.”

The answer left him with absolute certainty.

For the first time, something moved in the figure’s expression.

It was not approval.

“That was not the question.”

The words struck harder than the damaru.

The fracture tore open.

Darkness poured through it—not empty darkness, but movement layered upon movement. Shapes collided behind a surface thinner than skin. The voices became a single scream.

Samyukt pulled again.

The bow rose from the ash.

Violet light raced along its limbs. A string formed between them, narrow and bright enough to cut the night.

Samyukt turned towards the opening.

There was no arrow.

He drew anyway.

Something gathered against his fingers. Heat, sound and pressure folded into a single point. The scream beyond the sky answered it.

Before he could release, the meditating figure struck the damaru once.

Samyukt woke sitting upright.

His left arm was extended before him. His right hand was drawn beside his face, fingers closed around a string that was not there.

The room was dark. The fan turned overhead. Sweat had soaked through his shirt, and his heart struck painfully against his ribs.

His phone vibrated on the bedside table.

New anomalous seismic event recorded.

Samyukt lowered his hands.

A news application had attached an audio conversion of the latest pulse. He pressed play.

Static filled the room.

Then came the low mechanical vibration he had heard on television.

Beneath it, almost too deep to separate from his own heartbeat, sounded the rhythm of a damaru.

Samyukt looked towards the canvas case near the cupboard.

For the first time, the disturbance did not feel like something approaching.

It felt like something calling him.
