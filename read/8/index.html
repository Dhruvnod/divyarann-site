Chapter Eight

Sankalpa

संकल्प

The thing that had fallen three streets away stood up, and Samyukt’s first coherent thought was about his mother.

Not the sky. Not the tear in it, or the dark behind the tear, or the shape that had come through it like a stone through paper and was now rising out of its own dust with a slowness that was worse than speed. His mind went past all of that in a single step and arrived at a two-bedroom flat seven minutes away, where Naina would be in the kitchen and Ishita would be at the dining table with her entrance papers, and the window would be bowing inward the way the shop door had bowed, and neither of them would know what it meant.

He did not know what it meant either. That did not matter. He had learned at nineteen that not knowing was not an excuse.

“Keys,” he said.

The packer stared at him. The contractor had stopped screaming and was now standing in the middle of the shop with his hands over his ears, which was no use to anyone.

Samyukt found the motorcycle keys himself, in the drawer under the ledger, and was halfway to the door when it opened from outside and Kabir came through it.

He was breathing like a man who had run from his office, which was eleven minutes away, in six. His backpack was on his chest instead of his back. Behind him, at the kerb, Raghav was climbing out of a car he had abandoned diagonally across two parking spaces with the engine still running.

“The road is finished,” Raghav said, coming in. “Nothing is moving past the junction. We came round by the flyover.”

“Maa and Ishita.”

“I know.”

“The flat.”

“I know. We’re going.” Raghav looked at the keys in Samyukt’s hand. “Not on that. Three of us on a bike through that crowd and we’ll be under it in a minute. We run.”

Kabir had gone past them both to the back of the shop. He came back with two lengths of galvanised pipe from the pallet rack and the crowbar that lived behind the drums, and he held them out without comment, the way he would have handed over a screwdriver.

Samyukt took the crowbar. The weight of it was absurd. He thought of the thing standing up in the dust and the weight became more absurd, and he kept it anyway, because the alternative was empty hands.

“Keys in the shutter,” he said to the packer. “Pull it down. Go to the metro station, Sector 12, and wait there. Take him.” He meant the contractor. “Don’t stop for the tempo.”

Then they ran.

◆ ◆ ◆

The market had emptied in the wrong direction.

People were running east, away from the dust column, which was sensible, and they were running through the junction where every car had stopped and every driver had got out, which was not. The road was a maze of open doors. Samyukt went through it the way he had gone through the gaming maps at night, reading the gaps before he reached them, and twice Raghav caught his sleeve and steered him towards a gap before it closed.

The third time, Raghav said, without turning his head, “Left. Bike.”

Samyukt heard the engine a heartbeat later, hidden behind the horns and screaming. He went left, and a motorcycle with three men on it came through the gap where he had been and did not stop.

There was no time to say anything.

The pressure from above had not let up. It pressed on his shoulders like hands. Under it, the heat beneath his ribs had become something else, not heat now but a kind of pull, as if a hook had been set behind his sternum and the line ran up into the torn sky and somebody up there was taking in the slack.

He ran with his hand pressed to the spot, and ran, and did not stop.

They reached the flat in twelve minutes. He knew because he looked at the shop clock when they left and at the wall clock when he came through the door, and both would stay fixed in him for the rest of his life: eleven fourteen, eleven twenty-six.

Naina was standing in the hall with her handbag over her shoulder and her shoes on. Ishita was behind her with the canvas bow case in one hand and the kitchen torch in the other, and the look on her face said that she had decided to carry the bow and had not yet worked out why.

“You came,” Naina said.

“Of course I came.”

“The phones—”

“I know. We go to the metro. Now.” He was already turning her by the shoulders. “Raghav’s plan. The pillars.”

“Bhai.” Ishita held up the case. “I didn’t know what to—”

“Bring it.” He had no idea why he said it. “Come on.”

They went down the stairs with half the building, and in the stairwell a woman was shouting a name over and over, and Samyukt could not tell if it was a child’s name or a husband’s, and he did not stop to find out, and that was the first of the things he would carry.

◆ ◆ ◆

The family was in a doorway at the foot of the lane, where the lane met the sector road.

A man of thirty or so, in an office shirt with the sleeves rolled, and a woman in a salwar kameez with dust in her hair, and between them, held by one hand each, a girl of five with two plaits and a schoolbag still on her back. They were not running. They were standing in the doorway of a shuttered pharmacy as if the doorway were a decision they had made and could not unmake.

The man saw Samyukt coming and his face did a thing that Samyukt recognised, because he had seen it in the mirror at nineteen in a hospital car park. It was the face of someone looking for an adult.

Samyukt stopped.

Behind him he heard Raghav say his name, low, the way you say a name to someone standing at the edge of a platform.

“Where are you going?” Samyukt asked the man.

“We don’t— The school called, they said come and get her, and then the sky—” The man’s hand tightened on the girl’s. “We were going home. Our building is near the market.”

“Don’t go to the market.”

“But—”

“The metro station. Sector 12. Come with us. There are pillars. There are people.” He crouched, so his face was level with the girl’s. She had a cartoon sticker on her schoolbag strap and she was not crying, which was somehow worse than if she had been. “What’s your name?”

“Tara.”

“Tara. I’m Samyukt. We’re going somewhere safe. You hold your mother’s hand and your mother holds mine, and nobody lets go. All right?”

She nodded.

He stood and looked at the man, and the woman, and said the thing that he would hear himself say for the rest of his life, in the voice he used with suppliers.

“Come with us. I’ll get you out. Everything will be fine.”

The man’s name was Dinesh. The woman’s was Priya. Samyukt learned them in the next two hundred metres and would remember them for considerably longer.

◆ ◆ ◆

By the time they reached the market, they were not five people and a family. They were sixty, and then a hundred, and then he stopped counting.

It happened the way crowds happen: not by decision but by current. People came out of lanes and stairwells and parked cars and saw a group that was moving with purpose and joined it, and the group became a column, and the column became the thing that everyone else joined because it was already there. Raghav moved through it like a man who could see the whole board. He went ahead, came back, sent people left, held people right, and never raised his voice, and Samyukt watched the crowd shape itself around his friend without understanding why it obeyed.

Kabir had taken the rear. He walked backwards half the time, with his pipe across his shoulders like a yoke, and his eyes never left the sky.

The route to the station ran south along the sector road, past the market, under the metro corridor and into the lane that led to the concourse. Fifteen minutes on an ordinary day. The bottom of the road was the problem. That was where the first thing had fallen.

They saw it from ninety metres.

It was standing at the bottom of the sector road, where it met the metro corridor, beside the building under renovation, the long six-storey block that had been wrapped in green netting and bamboo scaffolding for a year, and it stood level with the fourth floor of the scaffolding.

Samyukt had seen this one come down, three streets from the shop, and begin to rise inside its own dust, and then he had stopped looking, because his mother was seven minutes away. The one he had seen whole was the one at the junction, in the first minutes, before Kabir came through the door: a hunched thing the size of a lorry, wrong-jointed, slag-coloured. He had thought they were the same. This was not that. This was to that what a building was to a brick. Four storeys of it. The same wrongness, the same dull sheen, the same face off a temple wall, but this face was the size of a first-floor balcony, under a broken bronze crown grown into the skull, and the arms, hanging, reached its knees. The scaffolding beside it shivered in a wind that was not blowing. Most of the glass on the renovation block had already gone.

The column stopped. A hundred people stopped as one body, the way a flock turns.

It had not seen them. It stood with its head turned slightly, in the attitude of something listening, and the air around it was heavy in a way Samyukt could feel from ninety metres: a bow-wave of pressure, a drawing-in. The green netting on the scaffold bellied towards it.

“Back,” Raghav said. “Back, back, there’s a lane behind the—”

The crowd behind them had not stopped. It was still arriving, still pushing, a hundred more people who had not yet seen what was in the road and who shoved into the backs of the hundred who had, and the column compressed like a spring. Someone screamed. Someone else screamed because the first one had. A metal shutter came down somewhere with a crash like a dropped sheet of iron.

The thing in the road turned its head.

“Oh no,” Kabir said, very quietly, at Samyukt’s shoulder. “No, no, no.”

It came in three steps.

Samyukt had time to see the steps and no time to do anything about them. Three bounding strides that ate thirty metres each, so fast that the dust rose behind the thing in a sheet, and then it was there, at the head of the column, at the mouth of the lane that led under the corridor to the station, and it brought one arm down into the road the way a child brings a hand down on an anthill.

The sound was not a sound. It was the ground.

He was on his back. He did not remember falling. The crowd had come apart around the impact like water around a stone, and people were on the ground everywhere, and the thing stood over the crater it had made with its arm still in it and raised its fanged face and made a noise that had no business coming from anything.

Naina. Where was—

She was behind him. Ishita had her. Ishita had the bow case in one hand and their mother in the other and her back against the renovation block’s side wall, and Dinesh and Priya and Tara were against the same wall three metres along, and the lane to the station was on the other side of the thing in the road.

There was no way round it. There was no way back; the crowd behind had become a wall of its own. There was the thing, and the lane behind it, and a hundred people in between with nothing in their hands.

Samyukt stood up.

He did not decide to. His body did it, the way it had put him in the car at nineteen before he had decided anything, and the crowbar was still in his hand because his hand had not let go of it, and he was walking forward.

“Samyukt!”

Raghav. Behind him. He did not turn.

Other men were getting up. He saw them without looking at them: a security guard with a lathi, two young men with the legs of a broken chair, a man his father’s age with nothing but a steel tiffin box held like a brick. They came forward because he was coming forward, and he understood with perfect clarity that if he stopped they would stop, and that none of them had the slightest chance.

The thing lifted its arm out of the crater.

He went in under it.

He hit it across the shin with the crowbar, with everything he had, with the swing he had learned at twelve from a man who had taught him to put his whole back into a bowstring, and the crowbar rang like a struck bell and bounced, and the shock of it went up both his arms into his shoulders and he nearly dropped it. The slag surface bore no mark. It had not noticed.

The security guard’s lathi broke on its other leg. One of the young men threw his chair leg and it clattered off the thing’s hip and fell. The man with the tiffin box got close enough to swing and the arm came down, not at him, past him, lazily, and he was simply gone, swept sideways into the shutters of a shop twenty metres away with a sound Samyukt would not let himself hear.

He hit it again. Again. The crowbar rang and rang.

It looked down at him.

He had never been looked at by anything that size. The face bent towards him, its eyes round and burning, and the pressure of its attention was physical, a weight, and in that weight he saw, perfectly, how this ended. The arm. The road. Naina against the wall with Ishita’s hand in hers. Tara with her sticker and her plaits. Dinesh looking for an adult. Raghav, who had lent him four lakh rupees and asked only that he stop being alone. Kabir, who had never once judged him.

Everything will be fine.

He had said it to his father, in the car, at every red light. He had said it to a five-year-old twenty minutes ago.

Something in him stopped arguing.

It was not courage. He would be clear about that, later, with the few people he told. Courage was a decision and he had made none. It was simpler than that and much worse. It was that every separate thing he was, the son who had not noticed in time and the brother who would not let his sister carry anything and the man who told everyone he wanted a quiet life, every one of them turned at the same moment towards the same single point, like filings, like water, and said the same thing.

This ends.

He did not think I will protect them, or I will not be helpless. Those were somewhere underneath. What he felt was simpler: a straight line from him to the thing in front of him, and at the end of it, an ending.

The heat under his ribs let go. For weeks it had brushed old pathways and withdrawn. Now the pathways opened.

It did not rise. It released, the way a drawn string releases, all at once, and went down both his arms in a flood so fast and so complete that his hands opened on their own and the crowbar fell. He heard it hit the road. He did not look at it. His left arm was coming up, straight, locked, the way it had come up ten thousand times on a shooting line between the ages of twelve and twenty-two, and his right hand was rising to his face, fingers hooked, to an anchor point under his jaw that his body had never forgotten.

He had nothing in his hands.

Then he did.

It built itself along his left arm from the fist outward, and it was black. Not painted black, not dark: black the way the gap in the sky was black, a black with depth, and through the depth, along seams like the joins between plates of stone, ran veins of violet light that moved. It had the shape of a bow because his hands were holding a bow, and it was taller than he was, and its limbs curved back on themselves at the tips the way the limbs of his old recurve curved, and it was not finished. He could see that it was not finished. Its edges were uncertain, as if drawn by someone remembering a bow rather than looking at one, and where the grip met his palm there was a smear of violet light the size of a fist that might have been a jewel if anyone had been given time to make it one.

A string came into being between the tips with a sound like a held breath.

He drew.

There was no arrow. He drew anyway, because his hand was at his anchor and his hand knew what came next, and between his hooked fingers something gathered, heat and sound and pressure folding into a single point, and the point lengthened, and it was an arrow, violet, so bright it had no edges, and it was screaming.

It screamed the way nothing alive could scream. A long rising shriek like wire drawn across the sky, and it rose with the draw, and the thing in the road heard it, finally, and reared back from it, and in rearing exposed the whole long slag column of its throat and chest to a man on the ground with a bow.

Raise. Draw. Anchor.

The target was impossibly close. He could see the grain of the thing, a crack in its surface like the crack in a dry riverbed, lit deep inside like a coal, a flaw a hand’s breadth wide at the base of its throat. He could see the air around it bend. From somewhere very far off, a question came back to him in a voice like a struck drum, and he did not have time for it.

Release.

◆ ◆ ◆

He did not see the arrow leave. Nobody did. There was the scream, and then the scream was somewhere else, and the thing in the road had a hole in it where its throat had been, a hole with daylight in it, and the arrow was still going.

It went up. It went through the thing and up, along the line it had been given, into the torn sky, a thread of violet so straight it did not look like motion, and it did not stop and he could not have called it back if he had known how.

Then the ring.

It had travelled around the arrow as a sheath of pressure and light. At the point of impact it opened in every direction at once, violet at the core and gold at its edge. It took the thing in the road apart; the great slag body came to pieces along the wave front like a sandcastle in surf, and the pieces were dust before they reached the ground. It took every window the renovation block had left. It took the scaffolding, all six storeys of bamboo and green net, and laid it down across the road in a long slow collapse that sounded like a forest falling. It picked up a parked car at the kerb and set it down on its roof. It went through the crowd like a hand through grass, and a hundred people went down. The returning edge reached Samyukt and divided around him, close enough to lift his clothes without striking his body, as though the force recognised the hand that had released it. He had no time to understand why.

Then it was past, and the dust came down, and in the quiet after it he heard the renovation block groan.

Not fall. Groan. A long deep complaint of concrete that had been cracked for nine days by the pulses and shaken by the fall and struck now by this, and had reached the end of what it was prepared to absorb. A crack ran up its face from an exposed ground-floor column beside the side wall to the fourth floor, slowly, like a thing being drawn, and stopped.

He was still standing. That surprised him.

The bow was gone. He did not remember it going. His hands were empty and his arms were out in front of him in the shape of a shot, and when he tried to bring them down they did not come, and when he tried again they came all at once and he went with them.

The road hit his knees. Then his hands. The heat under his ribs was gone.

Not lowered. Gone. Where it had been for seven weeks there was a place with nothing in it, cold and clean and sealed like a door, and the pull that had hooked him since the sky tore had let him go, and in the sudden absence of it he understood what the heat had been holding up.

Everything else.

“Samyukt. Samyukt.” Raghav, above him. Hands under his arms. “I’ve got him. Kabir, I’ve got— Aunty, stay there, stay with Ishita, don’t—”

He tried to say Tara. He tried to say the family, the wall, the building is going to, and what came out was breath.

Dust in his mouth. The road, very close, with a crack in it. Someone’s torch rolling. Naina’s voice saying his name the way she had said his father’s in the car, and he wanted to tell her it was not the same, he was not the same, he was only tired, and he could not find his mouth.

The last thing he saw, before the dark came in from the edges and closed, was the renovation block, through the settling dust, with the long crack up its face and the people against its side wall just beginning to understand that they should move.

Then nothing.

The arrow was still going.
